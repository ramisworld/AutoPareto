\title{AutoPareto: Auditing Fixed-Time Autonomous Transformer Search\\with Matched-Exposure Verification}
\author{Rami Alobaidy\\ \texttt{rameonix@gmail.com}}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Autonomous research agents can design and optimize machine learning systems, but the goals we give them shape what they discover. We introduce AutoPareto, a multi-objective research framework built on Andrej Karpathy's autoresearch, in which coding agents modify a Transformer's architecture, its training procedure and, in a later campaign, its inference code. Rather than optimizing language modelling quality alone, AutoPareto scores quality, generation speed and memory, and every candidate trains for the same fixed GPU time. A faster program therefore also trains on more data. Across independent campaigns, agents from two model families discovered that smaller Transformers and smaller batches fit substantially more training into the same time. Starting from a model with 50 million parameters, AutoPareto found a 29-million-parameter design that decodes nearly twice as fast as the quality-only autoresearch reference while using 35\% less inference memory. In a later campaign with its own benchmark, a Claude Opus 5.5 agent found Transformers with 22 to 26 million parameters and rewrote their generation code with the established techniques of CUDA graph replay and kernel fusion, reaching approximately 32 times the scored decode-only throughput of its registered baseline. Pre-registered retraining on new random seeds showed why the time budget favoured these designs: they processed more data within the time limit but did not learn more from each training token. Autonomous agents can thus discover substantial system efficiencies, and the objectives and budgets we set shape which solutions they pursue.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} autonomous ML research; neural architecture search; Transformer efficiency; multi-objective optimization; reproducibility

\section{Autonomous research result}\label{partone}

\subsection{The question}\label{sec:question}
\looseness=-1 In Karpathy's autoresearch \citep{autoresearch}, an AI agent repeatedly edits a language model's training program, trains each version for the same 300\,s on one GPU and keeps an edit only if the model improves. Because the budget is time, a faster program also trains on more data. AutoPareto keeps this loop but gives the agent three objectives: quality, decoding speed and inference memory. \textbf{We ask what agents discover, how, and whether the gains survive once speed and training data are decoupled.}

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Terms.} Quality is \emph{bits per byte} (BPB) on unseen text, lower is better: agents saw \emph{validation BPB}, and we confirm on \emph{held-out BPB} from a shard no agent saw. Halving the batch doubles the \emph{optimizer updates} for the same training tokens. \emph{Training throughput} counts training tokens per second and \emph{decode throughput} generated tokens per second; we never compare the two (glossary: Appendix~\ref{app:glossary}).

\subsection{How AutoPareto works}\label{sec:brief}
\looseness=-1 \textbf{The human sets the rules.} The task is to train a small Transformer language model on web text (ClimbMix) for 300\,s on one NVIDIA A40, starting from 8 layers, width 512 and 50.3M parameters. Candidates are scored on validation BPB, decode throughput (batch size one) and peak generation memory. The agent may edit only \texttt{train.py}, which holds the model, optimizer and training loop but no generation code; in every AutoPareto campaign a documented hook let the agent add its own, which the protected benchmark then timed (Appendix~\ref{app:access}).

\looseness=-1 \textbf{The agent runs the loop} (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:loop}). In each \emph{attempt} it reads all earlier attempts, picks a \emph{parent} recipe, records a hypothesis and expected trade-off, and edits \texttt{train.py}; a controller checks, trains, measures and records the result, failures included. A recipe is on the \emph{Pareto frontier} if no other is at least as good on all three objectives and better on one, so it need not win everywhere.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Two phases, eight approaches.} The \emph{original experiments} (July to August 2026) compared AutoPareto with GPT-5.6 against a quality-only AutoResearch agent and five classical optimizers tuning 15 fixed settings (random search, TPE, MOTPE, NSGA-II, CMA-ES; Appendix~\ref{app:approaches}), timing decoding with 256 input and 256 generated tokens. The \emph{extended experiments} (September to November 2026) ran GPT-5.6 and Claude Opus 5.5 under a revised harness, internally Pair-A, with one starting model, seed, program and benchmark (1{,}024 input, 128 generated tokens), and piloted equal-token training (\S\ref{sec:pilot}). Conditions differ, so we rank neither agents nor methods \citep[cf.][]{ferreira}.

\subsection{What the agents discovered}\label{sec:discover}
\looseness=-1 Verified recipes were retrained on new seeds for exactly 201{,}326{,}592 tokens (201.3M). \emph{AR-ref} is AutoResearch's best recipe that passed the cache check (8 layers, 50.3M parameters). \emph{AP-Q} (formerly AP292) is the quality-focused one of three AutoPareto representatives (fast AP-F and small AP-S: Table~\ref{tab:main}). \emph{Opus A23, A24 and A27}, attempts 23, 24 and 27, are Opus's whole final frontier. Random search, NSGA-II and CMA-ES were never retrained and have no verified point (Table~\ref{tab:approaches}).

AP-Q has 4 layers and 29.4M parameters, trains \CREightTrainRatio$\times$ as many tokens per second as AR-ref, decodes \CREightDecodeRatio$\times$ as fast (\CREightAPDecode{} against \CREightARDecode{} tokens per second) and needs \CREightMemRed\% less inference memory.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Opus found a frontier near 4{,}600 decoded tokens per second} (bottom). A23, A24 and A27 decode \CROpAbsTwentyThree, \CROpAbsTwentyFour{} and \CROpAbsTwentySeven{} tokens per second on a fresh A40: \CROpDecodeHi$\times$, \CROpDecodeLo$\times$ and \CREightOpTwentySevenX$\times$ the scored decode-only throughput of the registered baseline, run at \CRPTwoBaseLo{} to \CRPTwoBaseHi{} in the same session. A23 has the largest speed-up, A27 the highest rate, A24 the least memory (\CROpMemLo\,MiB) and parameters (\CREightOpTwentyFourParams M); rates differ by \CROpAbsSpreadPct\%, speed-ups mainly through the baseline runs. \textbf{No recipe wins everything}: AR-ref has the best held-out BPB in both seed groups, and AP-Q's \CREightAPDecode{} and A23's \CROpAbsTwentyThree{} tokens per second come from different benchmarks and form no ratio.

\subsection{How they discovered it}\label{sec:search}\label{sec:compare}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/cr_tracks.pdf}
\caption{Four searches. A line joins a recipe to the earlier recipe the agent started from (recorded parent). ``val'': validation BPB of a 300\,s run; $\times$: decode speed-up. Full lineages: Figures~\ref{fig:opusfull}, \ref{fig:lineagefull}.}\label{fig:tracks}\label{fig:opus}
\end{figure}

\looseness=-1 \textbf{AutoResearch} (Figure~\ref{fig:tracks}, left) never changed the model's size. Shorter attention windows, ``converting redundant attention compute into more training tokens'' (\#4), and two batch halvings cut validation BPB from \CREightARFour{} to \CREightARSix. \CREightARFail{} of its 50 runs failed, mostly at a cache check later found faulty; AR-ref comes from a 5-attempt follow-up (\S\ref{sec:interventions}).

\looseness=-1 \textbf{AutoPareto with GPT-5.6} cut the batch to a quarter (A1), expecting ``roughly four times as many optimizer updates within the fixed training window'', its largest gain ($-$\CREightAPOneGain). Depth cuts followed until A8 (4 layers, width 256) lost \CREightAPEightWorse; the agent restored width 512 (A18) and tuned learning rates into AP-Q (A22), decoding \CREightAPQDisc{} tokens per second in discovery against the baseline's \CREightAPBaseDisc.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Opus} reasoned that ``The model is therefore optimizer-update-limited, not token-limited'', halved the batch (A1) and cut depth one step at a time to 6, 4 and 3 layers (A3 to A5). Two layers (A6) lost \CREightOpSixWorse; noting that ``the quality prediction was falsified'', Opus returned to A5 and halved the batch again (A7, validation BPB \CREightOpSevenVal). Its \CRThreeLateValid{} later valid attempts gained only \CRThreeLateGain{} BPB. In A8 it wrote its own inference adapter, planning that ``If this candidate is valid, CUDA-graph decode will be the next isolated speed change.'' A10 replayed a CUDA graph because ``Batch-1 cached decode in attempt 8 is launch-bound'' (\CREightOpTenX$\times$); compiled fusion (A14, \CREightOpFourteenX$\times$), a ring K/V cache (A18, \CREightOpEighteenX$\times$), custom decode attention (A19, \CREightOpNineteenX$\times$) and a fused decode step (A22, \CREightOpTwentyTwoX$\times$) followed.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Same access, different strategies.} Both phases' programs listed the hook for the agent's own generation code as part of the research surface (Appendix~\ref{app:access}). GPT-5.6 never used it in 75 original or \CRCxAttempts{} extended attempts and expected a batch change to keep ``the architecture-dependent decode speed''; its extended gains came from smaller models (at most \CREightCxMaxX$\times$; best recipe A14: validation BPB \CREightCxBest, \CREightCxBestX$\times$) until a sandbox failure stopped the run. The arms also differed in reasoning setting, isolation, call timeout (raised to 900\,s after Opus's A9 calls timed out writing an adapter), repairs and attempts, and each ran once: two searches, not two model families.

\subsection{Did the discoveries hold up?}\label{sec:verify}
\looseness=-1 \textbf{Why equal tokens.} A smaller model trains faster and so sees more data in 300\,s; equal tokens ask which model learns more per token, equal time which is better in practice. The submitted panel (seeds 3 to 5) changed seeds, data and exposure at once. \textbf{E1}, pre-registered on 2026-11-04, varies only the budget: frozen AP-Q and AR-ref at 88.6M, 157.3M and 201.3M tokens with equal updates, and at 300\,s, on seeds 6 to 8 (24 runs). A second plan (2026-11-08) retrained A23, A24 and A27 at 201.3M tokens on those seeds, within 3\% of their 300\,s exposure.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Equal tokens: the quality gains do not survive.} AP-Q is worse than AR-ref at every budget by \CREOneMatchedLoAbs{} to \CREOneMatchedHiAbs{} held-out BPB, and for each seed, with seed SDs at most \CREOneMaxSD{} (Figure~\ref{fig:e1}), contradicting the pre-registered prediction that AP-Q is no worse at discovery scale. Opus's recipes are worse than AR-ref by \CRMTDLoAbs{} to \CRMTDHiAbs{} and than AP-Q by \CRFiveMTDAPLoAbs{} to \CRFiveMTDAPHiAbs.

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Equal time: no resolved difference.} At 300\,s AP-Q trains on \CREOneTokRatio$\times$ AR-ref's tokens and the held-out difference, \CREOneTimeHeldThree{} \CREOneTimeHeldCI, is not resolved, which is not evidence of equality; an exploratory split credits AP-Q's extra tokens with \CREOneExposure{} BPB and charges its architecture \CREOneArch{} (Figure~\ref{fig:e1split}).

\looseness=-1 \textbf{Downstream tasks} (Table~\ref{tab:verify}). AR-ref leads held-out BPB and BLiMP, AP-Q and A27 share the best ARC-Easy mean, A23 has the best PIQA and A27 the best LAMBADA mean; the seeds 3 to 5 panel repeats the AR-ref and AP-Q pattern. BLiMP tracks held-out BPB (panel $r=\CRCorrBLiMPHeld$); the other tasks barely separate recipes. A smaller model can lead on one task, through architecture, optimization, exposure, noise or the task itself; the records do not tell which.

\subsection{Why Opus decodes about 32$\times$ faster but generates only 5.3$\times$ faster}\label{sec:inference}
\looseness=-1 A model reads the prompt once (\emph{prefill}), caching keys and values, then generates one token per \emph{decode} step; the score times only the 127 decode steps after a 1{,}024-token prompt. At batch size 1 a small model's decode step is dominated by the CPU launching GPU programs (\emph{kernels}), \CRIAKernBase{} per token at the start. A \emph{CUDA graph} records them once and replays them with one call; \emph{kernel fusion} merges compatible operations, so \CRIAKernEager{} kernels become \CRIAKernFused.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/cr_inference.pdf}
\caption{Pre-registered ablations of A23, A24 and A27 in one session (medians; whiskers: range).}\label{fig:inference}
\end{figure}

\looseness=-1 \textbf{What the ablations show} (Figure~\ref{fig:inference}a, \CRIARuns{} runs). Without graph replay decoding is \CRIAGraphLo{} to \CRIAGraphHi$\times$ slower and without fusion \CRIAFusionLo{} to \CRIAFusionHi$\times$ slower; the effects interact and cannot be multiplied, and the smaller model alone gives \CRIAArchLo{} to \CRIAArchHi$\times$. As submitted the recipes reach a median \CRThreeDecX$\times$ in this session and \CROpDecodeLo{} to \CROpDecodeHi$\times$ in the earlier primary re-measurement, a separate session with its own baseline runs. \textbf{What the score does not time} (Figure~\ref{fig:inference}b). Opus captured the graph inside prefill (``Prefill time rose from 6.4 ms to 81 ms'', A11): prefill grows from \CRThreePreBase{} to \CRThreePreOpus{} ms while decoding shrinks from \CRThreeDecBase{} to \CRThreeDecOpus{} ms, so a whole generation is \CRThreeEtoEX$\times$ faster, not \CRThreeDecX$\times$. Correctness checks on 64- and 256-token prefixes cover neither the timed window nor the 1{,}024-token ring cache, and about 32$\times$ on one workload and GPU type need not hold elsewhere.

\subsection{What this teaches us}
\looseness=-1 Every agent pulled the speed lever of the 300\,s contract, which bought no better learning per token (\S\ref{sec:verify}). The efficiency is real: AP-Q decodes \CREightDecodeRatio$\times$ faster than AR-ref, and Opus, with CUDA graph replay and kernel fusion, reached about 32 times the baseline's scored decode-only throughput (\CROpDecodeHi$\times$), \CROpEtoEHi$\times$ for a full generation. Time budgets reward models that see more data and decode-only scores reward unscored prefill work: agents find what the contract and measurement boundary make attractive, so both belong to the result.

\subsection{Research Loop}\label{sec:loop}
% P2-04: restructured in revision 6 by Claude; the author stated on 2026-11-15 that Part 2 is reviewed and approved. Revision 7 changed names only; the author approved them on 2026-11-15 (camera_ready/CHANGELOG_CAMERA_READY.md).
\humanauthor[P2-04 r6]{Each attempt follows the eight steps below. The agent acts in steps 2 and 8 and, in original runs, labels the outcome in step 7. Protected code does everything else (Appendix~\ref{app:repro} names the functions).
{\small\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}\setlength{\topsep}{2pt}
\item \emph{Controller:} show the agent this campaign's records, the current frontier and the parent's source.
\item \emph{Agent:} return a hypothesis, the intended change, a change category, the chosen parent and a patch to \texttt{train.py}.
\item \emph{Controller:} validate. The parent must be on the current frontier (original) or parent-eligible in the same arm (extended). Protected files and the source must match their SHA-256 hashes, and only \texttt{train.py} may change.
\item \emph{Controller:} train under the contract, 300\,s or, in the extended fixed-token arm, $N^\star$ counted tokens.
\item \emph{Controller:} verify the checkpoint, measure validation BPB, check that cached decoding matches a full recomputation, and measure decode throughput and peak allocated memory.
\item \emph{Controller:} append an immutable record of the hypothesis, patch, hashes, metrics and any failure text. A failure still uses up the attempt.
\item \emph{Controller and agent:} update the archive. In the original experiments a completed candidate was retained unless another completed candidate or the registered same-seed baseline dominated it on raw BPB, decode speed and memory, with no noise margin. The agent labelled each outcome, and the runner rejected any label that disagreed with this rule.
\item \emph{Agent:} choose the next parent among the retained (original) or parent-eligible (extended) records.
\end{enumerate}}
Without a noise margin the archive keeps noise-level trade-offs. In AP-Q's campaign A9 and A10 build the same model, yet A10 was retained. In the extended experiments every quality-valid record was parent-eligible even when its speed was uncertain.}

The check itself was faulty. After it was repaired I discarded attempts 5--8, because they had followed a frontier that wrongly excluded attempt 4 (ids 88--90 are absent). \textbf{(ii) AutoResearch reference.} In the 50-attempt AutoResearch campaign, a cache-correctness check later found faulty discarded 27 trained candidates. AR-ref comes from a 5-attempt follow-up under a revised program. This is one reason AR-ref differs from our campaigns in more than the objective (\S\ref{sec:compare}).

\textbf{(iii) Extended Codex arm.} Pod and provider failures forced requeues, the inference adapter was amended, the configuration changed before launch, and a later restart adjudicated failures inconsistently. We use this arm only as a second instance of the speed lever, never to rank agents.

\textbf{(iv) Extended Opus arm.} A defect in the protected FP32 correctness adjudication was fixed mid-campaign and the affected attempt re-run. After three consecutive agent-call timeouts at attempt 9, I raised the call timeout from 600 to 900\,s for the rest of the arm (Codex ran at 600\,s). Attempt 9 was charged as a candidate failure, and timed-out calls made before any candidate were requeued. The arm was extended from 25 attempts to 25 valid results (27 attempts). Both arms moved to a second A40 once, Opus after A1 and Codex after A9. We also report the frozen first 25 attempts, and neither the timeout nor the extension changes a conclusion (Appendix~\ref{app:faq}).

\textbf{(v) Failure accounting.} The same tokenizer failure was counted differently in two campaigns. In seed 0 it consumed attempt A17, while in seed 42 it was relabelled as a setup failure. AP-Q's campaign thus carries the stricter accounting.

\textbf{(vi) E1 hardware.} The A40 on which E1's environment check (E0) passed was no longer available, so E1 ran on another A40 with the same driver. The deviation is recorded, and throughput matched E0 (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\textbf{(vii) Opus retraining.} Opus had rewritten the training-loop driver for the time contract, so its sources could not run under a token budget unchanged. A scripted driver mapping and a storage export, pre-registered with the plan, made them runnable while keeping model and optimizer code byte-identical (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

The AP-Q lineage (seed 0) has no recorded scientific intervention: its program, evaluator and records were not changed. Interventions whose authorship cannot be reconstructed are excluded from claims of autonomous contribution.}

\subsection{Verification}\label{sec:verification}
\humanauthor[P2-07 r6]{A number can be wrong without any run failing: a result may come from the wrong script, a run may see the wrong number of tokens, or an evaluation may use another run's checkpoint. A separate audit pipeline therefore re-derives each reported number from the frozen records, without launching experiments, and checks six things.
{\setlength{\leftmargini}{1.2em}\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}\setlength{\topsep}{1pt}
\item \emph{The code behind each model.} For each of the 21 panel runs the hash of the training source must match the training record and the public manifest.
\item \emph{Tokens and updates.} Each run must process the exact token total, with updates equal to tokens divided by tokens per update, so recipes compared at equal tokens really saw equal tokens.
\item \emph{Checkpoints and evaluations.} Each downstream result must come from its own run's checkpoint, and every metric must point in its stated direction.
\item \emph{Reported results.} Opus's frontier was re-measured on a fresh A40, where protected BPB reproduced bit for bit, and an AP-Q checkpoint reproduced all nine August downstream metrics. \CRNCrossChecked{} panel quantities agree with an independent audit to $10^{-15}$.
\item \emph{Hypotheses and parents.} Scripts check every quoted agent excerpt verbatim against its attempt record and every drawn lineage edge against a recorded parent link.
\item \emph{Statistics.} E1 was analysed by its pre-registered script, the exploratory split reproduces its paired differences exactly, and the Opus tests' pre-registered analysis reproduces byte for byte from the archived raw records.
\end{itemize}}
Every result number in Part~1, every table and every data figure is generated by scripts in \texttt{analysis/}. Figure~\ref{fig:loop}, the glossary and the discovery and canary environment rows are hand-authored. Data-integrity checks are in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.}

\humanauthor[P2-09 r6]{\textbf{Selection and the winner's curse.} Each discovery score comes from one noisy run. When the best of many noisy scores is picked, the winner tends to be a recipe that was also lucky, so its score overstates its quality. As this winner's curse predicts, every re-run candidate scored \CROptMin--\CROptMax{} BPB worse than at discovery. This is why we retrained the selected recipes independently, on new seeds and a held-out shard, instead of trusting their discovery scores. Selection happened in stages (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:funnel}). The representative rule was committed on 2026-07-27, before AutoPareto's first attempt, and yields 11 representatives. On 2026-08-10, after discovery, a global four-role selector cut these to four seed-0 recipes. The confirmation campaigns were created on 2026-08-11/12 UTC, so selection preceded the seeds-0--2 confirmation. The 201M panel was assembled around 2026-08-21--25, after those results were known, and kept three of the four roles. No rule was recorded for omitting the lowest-memory role (exp 124). \textbf{Not confirmed.} Exp 109, the best seed-42 recipe, keeps the full 50.3M-parameter model at batch 65,536 and reached \CRExpOneZeroNineBPB{} on \CRExpOneZeroNineTokens M tokens (AR-ref: \CRSeedFortyTwoAR{} on \CRExpSixEightTokens M). It was a pre-registered representative but was never confirmed, and it is the most plausible AutoPareto quality candidate that does not rely on extra exposure. E1 tests AP-Q, not the best possible AutoPareto recipe, and our conclusions do not extend to unconfirmed recipes.}

\humanauthor[P2-10 r6]{\textbf{Variability.} Within the agent campaigns the best recipe beats the second by at most \CRGapTwoMax{} BPB and the fifth by at most \CRGapFiveMax, while the pooled fixed-time seed SD is \CRPooledSD. A campaign's ``best'' recipe is therefore not resolvable from its runners-up. The unchanged baseline differs by \CRBaseSeedEffect{} BPB between seeds 0 and 42, and by \CRSessionLo--\CRSessionHi{} BPB at the same seed between sessions. Under the fixed-time contract one recipe processed up to \CRTokenSpreadMax\% different token counts across sessions. In E1 the within-recipe seed SD is at most \CREOneMaxSD{} BPB, an order of magnitude below the matched-token gap. Speed-measurement noise in extended runs and in the ablations is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.}

\subsection{Significance of result}
\humanauthor[P2-12 r6]{Matched-exposure verification shows that the quality ranking depends on the contract, while the efficiency gains hold under every contract we tested. At no pre-registered token budget is AP-Q better per token, so its fixed-time standing reflects its extra exposure, not better learning. Opus pushed the same lever further: its recipes were worse still per token, and most of its decode gain came from graph replay whose recording the metric did not time. A benchmark that ranks agents under a time budget therefore partly ranks how fast their candidates train. Quality gains should be rechecked at controlled exposure, and speed gains with end-to-end timing.}

\subsection{Interpretability}
\humanauthor[P2-13 r6]{Every preserved attempt record keeps the agent's hypothesis, intended change, applied patch, parent, source and parent hashes, status, raw metrics and failure text. A headline number can therefore be traced to a source file, to the edit that produced it and to the hypothesis recorded before the run (Figure~\ref{fig:opus}, Appendix~\ref{app:decisions}). We keep apart what the agent predicted, what it changed, what the evaluator measured and what our retrospective analysis concludes, and we quote agent motives rather than infer them. The records also expose gaps between intent and execution (Appendix~\ref{app:decisions}).}

\subsection{Limitations}
\humanauthor[P2-14 r6]{\emph{Addressed since review:} the confounded reversal, the single matched horizon and the fixed-time variability (E1 and above). \emph{Remaining:} one GPU type and small models, so we make no scale-transfer, cross-GPU or energy claim. Matched-token verification compares complete recipes, so architecture and optimizer changes stay bundled, and in E1 tokens and updates change together. E1 resolves the gap with three seeds but not its slope with tokens, and its exposure--architecture split is exploratory. A pre-registered full-size candidate (exp 109) was never confirmed. Each extended-experiment agent ran one seed under slightly different conditions (\S\ref{sec:interventions}), so we do not rank agents. The Opus retraining covers one budget and replaces time-fraction learning-rate progress with the token fraction. The ablations measure one workload on one host, and graph replay and fusion were not applied to the baseline or AR-ref. The original comparators differ in search space, budget, seed and failure history, so they support no search-policy ranking.}

\subsection{Extended experiments: the contract pilot}\label{sec:pilot}
\humanauthor[P2-15 r6]{\textbf{The question.} Does the training contract change what an agent pursues? We piloted a prospective test of this hypothesis (H2). Two arms started from identical code at seed 3, with programs that differ only in the paragraph that states the contract. Arm T trains each candidate for 300\,s. Arm N trains each candidate on $N^\star$ counted tokens, set from baseline calibration runs (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). $N^\star$ was 88,604,672 in the first launches and 85,458,944 after recalibration on a new A40 from 2026-09-09. Five Codex launches ran between 2026-09-05 and 2026-09-17, with protocol changes between them. Part~\ref{partone} uses the fixed-time arm of the 2026-09-14 restart (22 attempts per arm).

\textbf{What the arms explored} (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pilot}). In each of the three launches in which the agent changed the architecture, the fixed-token arm's best model was larger: \CRFivePilotNA M against \CRFivePilotTA M parameters, \CRFivePilotNB M against \CRFivePilotTB M and \CRFivePilotNC M against \CRFivePilotTC M, or \CRFivePilotRatioLo{} to \CRFivePilotRatioHi{} times as large. Only fixed-token attempts grew the model, to 10 and 12 layers, expecting a better BPB ``by increasing model capacity''. Under fixed time the agent instead cut depth and width and trained on up to 175M tokens. Both arms reduced the batch, in different launches, so batch size does not separate them. Each launch ran each arm once with a shared seed and starting code, the harness changed between launches, and six fixed-time attempts were lost to the defect below. The pilot therefore shows an observed tendency, not an estimated effect of the contract.

\textbf{A harness defect.} In the lineage reported at submission, 2 of the first 4 fixed-time attempts are invalid. When a candidate's training loop ended before the trusted stop, the harness re-ran the training body and saved a freshly initialized model (step 0 reported against 1,264 observed updates, BPB 3.2159). The controller still marked these runs quality-valid and parent-eligible, and the agent's next hypothesis blamed its learning rates. The Opus harness treats such an early exit as a candidate failure.}

\humanauthor[P2-16 r6]{\textbf{What the speed metric does not time.} Opus recorded its decode CUDA graph, and later compiled regions, inside the untimed prefill (\S\ref{sec:inference}), which its program calls ``diagnostic''. The rules allowed this, so we report both speeds and recommend scoring prefill and checking outputs inside the timed windows.}

\section{Broader Impact}\label{partthree}
Resource contracts are part of the scientific specification of autonomous research. Neither contract is wrong: a time budget asks which program gives the best model per GPU-second, a token budget which gives the best model per training token, and a paper should say which question its scores answer. When an agent controls architecture and training together, a time allowance changes how much data a faster candidate sees. A token allowance removes that pathway while letting update count and compute vary. The measurement contract matters in the same way: an agent rewarded for a decode-only number will improve the decode-only number, here largely by moving graph recording into the untimed prefill. Agents read a contract the way an optimizer reads a loss: agents from both model families pulled the throughput lever within their first three attempts, and Opus moved work into the untimed prefill while noting its cost. Contract designers should assume that an agent will find every degree of freedom the contract leaves open. The contract may also steer what agents build: in our small pilot the same agent kept larger models under a fixed-token budget than under a fixed-time one. A replicated, seed-paired study could test whether this holds.

\textbf{A contract card.} We propose that autonomous-ML papers report, for every scored result:
{\setlength{\leftmargini}{1.6em}\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}\setlength{\topsep}{2pt}
\item the stopping rule and the learning-rate progress variable,
\item the seeds used for discovery and for comparison,
\item tokens, optimizer updates and FLOPs per scored run,
\item session-throughput variation under time contracts,
\item what each speed or memory metric times and excludes, such as prefill,
\item the selection funnel from attempts to reported recipes, with dates, and
\item harness invariants such as checkpoint lineage and failure semantics.
\end{enumerate}}
\noindent Our own harness once accepted a checkpoint of an untrained model as quality-valid. Invariant checks belong in the protected boundary, not in post-hoc audits.

\textbf{Future direction: agents that ask for their own confirmation.} Here humans noticed the coupling and ran matched-resource retraining after the search. A natural next step, which we have not built or tested, is to let the agent request it: before claiming a quality gain under a time contract, the agent would ask the harness for a matched-token retraining against its parent on fresh seeds, and an end-to-end timing before claiming a speed gain. Other open follow-ups are a batch-varied test that separates tokens from updates, a matched-token test of exp 109, a seed-paired contract study replicated under one frozen harness with prefill-inclusive speed scoring, and a scaling pilot.

Efficiency may reduce the cost of an individual experiment or deployment, yet cheaper experiments can increase total experimentation. We report no energy benefit because the artifacts contain no campaign-wide energy accounting. Small-model benchmark results do not establish safe or reliable use in consequential settings. Human accountability grows with experiment volume: humans set objectives, repair infrastructure, decide which failures are scientific and interpret evidence, and an auditable record should keep the human question, the agent's prediction, the source edit, the measured outcome and the retrospective explanation distinguishable.
\label{partthreeend}

\section{Glossary}\label{app:glossary}
\humanauthor[P2-01 Glossary]{\textbf{Bits per byte (BPB)}: average bits needed to encode each byte of unseen text under the model. Lower is better. \emph{Validation} BPB is the discovery score; \emph{held-out} BPB uses a separate pinned shard used only for confirmation. The two are never compared with each other.\par
\textbf{Contract}: the rule that stops training; \emph{fixed time} (300\,s of measured training) or \emph{fixed tokens} (a set number of counted training tokens).\par
\textbf{Exposure}: training tokens processed before evaluation. \textbf{Optimizer update}: one change of the weights from one batch of tokens.\par
\textbf{Matched-exposure retraining}: retraining frozen recipes to the same token count (for AP-Q, AR-ref and Opus's recipes also the same updates and batch).\par
\textbf{E1}: the pre-registered token-response experiment (seeds 6--8; 88.6M, 157.3M, 201.3M tokens and 300\,s).\par
\textbf{Campaign / attempt}: one agent's sequence of attempts under one seed. An attempt is one proposal (hypothesis and patch), trained and measured once. Failures count.\par
\textbf{Parent}: the archived attempt whose source an attempt edits. Original-experiment parents had to be retained, extended-experiment parents only quality-valid (\emph{parent-eligible}).\par
\textbf{Pareto frontier / retained}: recipes that no other recipe matches or beats on all of BPB, decode speed and memory while beating on one. In the extended experiments, speed-uncertain records are not on the frontier but remain parent-eligible.\par
\textbf{Autoregressive decoding, prefill, K/V cache}: generating one token at a time. Prefill processes the prompt once and fills the cache of attention keys and values so that each new token needs one small step.\par
\textbf{Decode-only vs end-to-end speed}: cached single-token steps after an untimed prefill, vs prefill plus generation.\par
\textbf{CUDA graph}: a recorded sequence of GPU kernels replayed with one CPU call. \textbf{Kernel fusion}: merging small GPU kernels into fewer, larger ones.\par
\textbf{Ablation}: removing one component to measure its effect. \textbf{95\% confidence interval (CI)}: a range computed from the data that expresses uncertainty about an effect. A CI spanning zero means the sign is not resolved.\par
\textbf{Recipe names}: AP-Q = A22 = exp 134 (AP292 in the submitted version); AP-F = A25 = exp 137 (AP299); AP-S = A24 = exp 136 (AP294); AR-ref = exp 68; Opus A23, A24, A27 = Opus attempts in the extended experiments; T and N are the fixed-time and fixed-token arms of the extended experiments.\par
\textbf{Original and extended experiments}: the July to August 2026 campaigns (GPT-5.6, AutoResearch, classical optimizers; decode benchmark of 256 input and 256 generated tokens) and the September to November 2026 campaigns under the revised Pair-A harness (GPT-5.6 and Opus; 1{,}024 input and 128 generated tokens).}

\section{Related work}\label{app:related}
Operator-level, grammar-based and multi-objective architecture search \citep{primer,einspace,mote,monaq}, multi-fidelity allocation \citep{hyperband,bohb} and agentic research systems \citep{autodiscovery,mlestar,airesearcher,mledojo} are established, as are budget effects on model rankings \citep{dehghani2022efficiency,geiping2023cramming,hoffmann2022chinchilla}. The classical comparators are random search, TPE \citep{bergstra2011tpe}, MOTPE \citep{ozaki2020motpe}, NSGA-II \citep{deb2002nsga2} and CMA-ES \citep{hansen2001cmaes} over a fixed parameter vector, and \citet{ferreira} compare LLM agents with such optimizers on autoresearch. Primer searches compositions of low-level operations and finds stronger operator-level novelty than our numerical edits. Our architectural changes (fewer layers, smaller batches, attention windows) are not novel in themselves. Our contribution is an audit of what a resource contract rewards, with matched-exposure verification. The zero-shot suite is ARC-Easy \citep{clark2018arc}, PIQA \citep{bisk2020piqa}, LAMBADA \citep{paperno2016lambada} and BLiMP \citep{warstadt2020blimp}.

\section{Who could edit the inference code}\label{app:access}
Every AutoPareto agent could write only \texttt{train.py}, and in every AutoPareto campaign that file could define \texttt{build\_inference\_adapter(model)}, a documented hook whose \texttt{prefill} and \texttt{decode\_one} the protected benchmark then timed in its own loop, with its own prompts, correctness checks and repetitions. The starting \texttt{train.py} of both phases contained no generation code. Without an adapter, the protected benchmark ran its own re-implementation of the model, so architecture edits changed the timed computation and edits to the candidate's forward code did not. The 50-attempt AutoResearch campaign predates the hook.

Prefill and end-to-end speed are recorded but not scored. Each measurement discards a warm-up and times 8 windows of about 8\,s. The candidate's rate is the median window rate, and its speed-up is the median of the 8 window ratios to baseline rates interpolated from windows measured before and after it. On the fresh-A40 re-measurement the raw repeat records give medians over three repeats of \CROpAbsTwentyThree, \CROpAbsTwentyFour{} and \CROpAbsTwentySeven{} tok/s for Opus A23, A24 and A27, with bracketing baseline rates of \CRPTwoBaseLo--\CRPTwoBaseHi{} tok/s. The ablation session measured \CRIAAbsLo--\CRIAAbsHi{} tok/s for the same recipes as submitted. Speed-ups are medians of window ratios, not ratios of these medians, and the registered baseline's calibration run decoded \CRAgBaseDecode{} tok/s. The three Opus recipes differ in absolute rate by \CROpAbsSpreadPct\%, so their speed-ups (\CROpDecodeLo$\times$ to \CROpDecodeHi$\times$) differ mainly through the baseline windows. In the extended experiments, \CROpUncertain{} of Opus's \CROpValid{} valid results and \CRCxUncertain{} of Codex's \CRCxValid{} were speed-uncertain. In the Opus arm this drift came from the baseline brackets on noisier hosts, not from the candidates. When Opus's frontier was re-measured, \CROpStableRepeats{} of \CROpRepeats{} repeats passed the drift rule. In the ablations \CRIAUnstable{} of \CRIARuns{} runs, all host-bound runs without the graph, failed the frozen stability rule and are reported, not re-run. Peak memory is \texttt{torch.cuda.max\_memory\_allocated()} after \texttt{reset\_peak\_memory\_stats()}, with weights resident and reserved memory excluded.

\section{Questions a reader may ask}\label{app:faq}
\textbf{Is AP-Q simply a worse model?} Per training token, yes (Table~\ref{tab:results}). Per GPU-second its difference from AR-ref is not resolved (300\,s, E1) while it is \CRParamReduction\% smaller, decodes \CRDecodeRatio$\times$ faster and uses \CRMemoryReduction\% less inference memory. Which matters depends on whether compute time or data is the scarce resource.

\textbf{Why not use a fixed-token contract from the start?} A fixed-time contract is the autoresearch standard and answers a practical question: the best model from a fixed amount of GPU time. It is not wrong, but it scores a program's throughput and its per-token quality together. A fixed-token contract isolates per-token quality but lets compute and update count vary. We report both.

\textbf{Why did every agent shrink the model?} Under 300\,s, BPB improves with more updates and tokens, and a smaller model gets more of both. The agents' hypotheses say so (Table~\ref{tab:reasoning}). E1 prices the trade for AP-Q: it trains \CREOneRho$\times$ faster but needs about \CREOneK$\times$ AR-ref's tokens for equal quality, so at 300\,s the two roughly cancel. Opus's recipes would need more than \CRMTKBound$\times$ AR-ref's tokens, about their speed advantage, and post hoc, at 201.3M tokens they do not differ resolvably from AR-ref at 300\,s (95\% CIs span zero).

\textbf{Does E1 prove that exposure is the only cause?} No. E1 shows that at equal tokens and updates AP-Q is worse, on the same seeds and data, and its exploratory split attributes the 300\,s result to extra tokens offsetting a weaker architecture. Tokens and updates move together, and architecture and optimizer edits are bundled, so finer causes are not separated.

\textbf{Are three seeds enough?} For the matched-token gap, yes: \CREOneMatchedLoAbs--\CREOneMatchedHiAbs{} held-out BPB against a within-recipe seed SD of at most \CREOneMaxSD, on all \CREOnePairs{} seed--budget pairs, and the panel result replicates on other seeds and another A40. For the slope of the gap with tokens, or to show equivalence at 300\,s, no.

\textbf{Did Opus game the decode metric?} It stayed within the permitted surface: the adapter had to compute the same outputs, and every variant passed the correctness checks with identical greedy tokens on the 64- and 256-token prefixes they cover. Those checks reach neither the timed window nor the 1{,}024-token ring cache, so full-workload equivalence is not established. Its records describe graph replay as a fix for launch-bound decode and note the prefill cost (Table~\ref{tab:reasoning}), so it was the metric's definition, not a rule violation, that rewarded the placement. We report decode-only and end-to-end speed side by side and recommend prefill-inclusive scoring.

\textbf{Is the Codex--Opus comparison fair?} The arms share the baseline, seed, prompt bytes, output schemas, contract, Pareto rule and the hook for the agent's own inference code (Appendix~\ref{app:access}). They differ in reasoning setting (high vs xhigh), agent-call timeout (600 vs 900\,s from Opus A10), isolation, harness repairs and attempt count (\CRCxAttempts{} vs \CROpAttempts), and each ran one seed. At \CRCxAttempts{} attempts each the best validation BPBs are \CRCxBestBPB{} and \CROpBestTwentyTwo. We draw no agent ranking. The arms show the same exposure strategy and supply recipes for retraining.

\textbf{Does the Opus extension to \CROpAttempts{} attempts change anything?} No. Its best validation BPB (\CROpBestFrozen) came within the frozen first 25 attempts. The frozen frontier (\CROpFrontierFrozen) shares Opus A23 and A24 with the retrained set, and the third retrained recipe, Opus A27, matches Opus A23's held-out gap to AR-ref to three decimals (Table~\ref{tab:results}).

\textbf{Could the time-to-token mapping handicap Opus's recipes?} The mapping changed only driver lines (clock, stop, progress). Model and optimizer code stayed byte-identical. The 201.3M tokens and 1{,}536 updates are within 3\% of Opus's own 300\,s runs (\CROpTokLo--\CROpTokHi M tokens, \CROpUpdLo--\CROpUpdHi{} updates), so its recipes were scored at almost the exposure they were tuned for, and an AR-ref drift control reproduced E1 within \CRMTDriftAbs{} BPB.

\textbf{Were the reported recipes cherry-picked?} The funnel and its dates are in Table~\ref{tab:funnel}: the representative rule predates the first attempt, the four-role selector preceded the seeds 0--2 confirmation, and the panel was assembled after it. A full-size candidate (exp 109) was never confirmed. AR-ref replaced exp 65, which failed a cache-correctness check under a reference later judged faulty, and their fixed-time re-runs do not differ resolvably (\CRFixedSixtyFive{} and \CRFixedSixtyEight). E1, the Opus retraining and the ablations were pre-registered, and the Opus retraining covers its whole frontier.

\textbf{Does this transfer to larger models or other GPUs?} Untested: one GPU type and recipes of 18--50M parameters.
