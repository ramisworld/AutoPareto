\title{AutoPareto: Auditing Fixed-Time Autonomous Transformer Search\\with Matched-Exposure Verification}
\author{Rami Alobaidy\\ \authortodo{affiliation: author to confirm}\\ \texttt{rameonix@gmail.com}}

\begin{abstract}
Autonomous ML research agents are usually scored under a fixed wall-clock budget, where a program that trains faster also sees more data before it is scored.
We audit what that rewards in AutoPareto, an agent loop that edits a small Transformer's training script and is scored on validation bits-per-byte (BPB), decode speed and inference memory on one NVIDIA A40.
Its best-quality recipe (AP-Q) halves the depth of a quality-only AutoResearch agent's reference recipe (AR-ref) and trains \CREOneRho$\times$ faster.
In a pre-registered test on fresh seeds, AP-Q is worse than AR-ref by \CREOneMatchedLoAbs--\CREOneMatchedHiAbs{} held-out BPB at every matched budget from 88.6M to 201.3M training tokens (all \CREOnePairs{} seed--budget pairs), yet ties it at equal 300\,s training time (\CREOneTimeHeldThree; 95\% CI \CREOneTimeHeldLo{} to \CREOneTimeHeldHi).
Claude Opus 5.5, run under the same contract, took the same route further: its frontier recipes train \CRMTRhoLo--\CRMTRhoHi$\times$ faster than AR-ref and, retrained at 201.3M tokens (pre-registered), are worse by \CRMTDLoAbs--\CRMTDHiAbs{} BPB.
Both agents' recorded hypotheses give the extra updates or tokens a smaller model gets in 300\,s as the reason to shrink it.
Opus's roughly \CRIATotalMed$\times$ decode-only speed-up over the baseline is mostly inference engineering: ablations attribute \CRIAJointLo--\CRIAJointHi$\times$ to CUDA-graph replay and kernel fusion and \CRIAArchLo--\CRIAArchHi$\times$ to the smaller model, and because the graph is captured in the untimed prefill, generation is only about \CRIAEtoEMed$\times$ faster end to end.
Efficiency survives both contracts: at matched tokens AP-Q decodes \CRDecodeRatio$\times$ faster than AR-ref with \CRParamReduction\% fewer parameters and \CRMemoryReduction\% less inference memory.
What a contract trains and what it times decide what an autonomous researcher is rewarded for.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} autonomous ML research; neural architecture search; Transformer efficiency; multi-objective optimization; reproducibility

\section{Autonomous research result}\label{partone}

\subsection{AutoPareto in brief}\label{sec:brief}
AutoPareto is an autonomous research loop for small language models. In each attempt an LLM coding agent reads its campaign's results so far, picks a parent training script, states a hypothesis and edits one file, \texttt{train.py}; a protected, hash-checked harness trains the edited model on one A40 under a fixed resource contract, measures validation BPB (quality), cached-decode tokens/s (speed) and peak inference memory, and appends every attempt, including failures, to a lineage archive (exceptions in \S\ref{sec:interventions}). A \emph{contract} is the rule that stops training: under the \emph{fixed-time} contract of every discovery campaign each candidate trains for 300\,s, so a faster program also processes more tokens; under a \emph{fixed-token} contract every candidate processes the same tokens. We report three 25-attempt historical campaigns, compared with a quality-only AutoResearch agent and five classical optimizers; two later fixed-time campaigns of different agents in one frozen harness; and pre-registered retraining and ablations that test what each agent's score was made of. Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} is a glossary; Appendix~\ref{app:faq} answers common questions.

\subsection{Hypothesis}
Under a fixed wall-clock budget a faster program processes more tokens before it is scored, so a discovery-stage score mixes the program edit with the resources the contract allocates to it.
\textbf{H1 (retrospective).} AP-Q's discovery-stage lead over AR-ref under the fixed-time contract reverses when both are retrained to equal training tokens on new seeds; H1 was formulated after inspecting the historical campaigns.
\textbf{Pre-registered tests.} We pre-registered a token-response plan (E1; SHA-256 \texttt{89f29015}, 2026-11-04) for $\Delta=\mathrm{BPB}(\text{AP-Q})-\mathrm{BPB}(\text{AR-ref})$: \emph{H-E1a}, $\Delta$ increases monotonically with training tokens; \emph{H-E1b}, at the discovery-scale budget (88.6M tokens, 676 updates) $\Delta\le0$. On 2026-11-08 we committed plans (\texttt{a47f81b7}, \texttt{94727990}) to retrain Opus's three frontier recipes at 201.3M tokens on E1's seeds (primary: each recipe's $\Delta$ against AR-ref) and to ablate their inference adapters. Part~2 pilots H2, whether the contract changes what an agent pursues (\S\ref{sec:pilot}).

\subsection{Method}
\paragraph{Task and discovery contract.} The registered baseline is an eight-layer, 512-wide decoder-only Transformer (50,332,176 parameters), trained on ClimbMix-400B-shuffle with an 8,192-token vocabulary and 2,048-token packed sequences on one NVIDIA A40. Each attempt edits \texttt{train.py} once, then runs protected training under a 300.000-second measured budget that stops after the first completed update at or beyond the boundary, following autoresearch \citep{autoresearch}. Historical campaigns used seeds 0, 1 and 42; seed effects on the unchanged baseline reach \CRBaseSeedEffect{} BPB (\S\ref{sec:verification}), so inferential comparisons use shared seeds only. Validation BPB (cross-entropy per byte of UTF-8 text) measures quality; cached-decode tokens/s measures speed (BF16, batch 1; single-token steps after an untimed prefill); peak allocated CUDA memory measures inference memory, weights included. We also report end-to-end speed, prefill included, where recorded (protocol: Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\paragraph{Comparators.} We compare with a quality-only AutoResearch agent (different prompt, budget and wrappers) and with random search, TPE, MOTPE, NSGA-II and CMA-ES \citep{bergstra2011tpe,ozaki2020motpe,deb2002nsga2,hansen2001cmaes} over a fixed parameter vector. Operator-level, grammar-based and multi-objective NAS \citep{primer,einspace,mote,monaq}, multi-fidelity allocation \citep{hyperband,bohb} and agentic research systems \citep{autodiscovery,mlestar,airesearcher,mledojo} are established, as are budget effects on model rankings \citep{dehghani2022efficiency,geiping2023cramming,hoffmann2022chinchilla}; we claim no firsts. At the shared seed 42 the best AutoPareto, AutoResearch, TPE and MOTPE recipes lie within \CRSeedFortyTwoSpread{} BPB; with archives differing in search space, budget and failures we draw no search-policy conclusion \citep[cf.][]{ferreira}.

\paragraph{Matched-exposure verification.} We froze seven recipes as exact source files and retrained each at seeds 3--5 for exactly \CRPanelBudget{} counted tokens (1{,}536 updates of 131{,}072 tokens; 384 of 524{,}288 for the baseline), with held-out BPB on a separate pinned shard (the \emph{panel}; Appendix~\ref{app:panel}). E1 retrained AP-Q and AR-ref at 88.6M, 157.3M and 201.3M tokens (676, 1{,}200 and 1{,}536 updates at batch 131{,}072) and for 300\,s on fresh seeds 6--8 (24 runs; held-out BPB primary; paired 95\% $t$ intervals, $n=3$). The same executor then retrained Opus's frontier at 201.3M tokens on seeds 6--8 against E1's runs, after mapping its time-based driver lines to the token-budget driver (scripted and bit-checked; Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). We also use the 300\,s re-runs on seeds 0--2 and a frozen zero-shot suite (ARC-Easy, PIQA, LAMBADA, BLiMP; \citealp{clark2018arc,bisk2020piqa,paperno2016lambada,warstadt2020blimp}) that no agent saw.

\paragraph{Two agents, one contract.} In a later controlled harness (Pair-A, \S\ref{sec:pilot}) the agent may also rewrite the inference adapter in \texttt{train.py}, while the protected evaluator owns timing and scores decode-only throughput (127 cached steps after an untimed 1{,}024-token prefill). From the same baseline, seed 3, prompt bytes, output schemas and Pareto rule, Codex CLI with \texttt{gpt-5.6-sol} ran \CRCxAttempts{} fixed-time attempts and Claude Opus 5.5 through Claude Code ran \CROpAttempts{} (\CROpValid{} valid); Opus's frontier was re-measured on a fresh A40.

\paragraph{Token response (E1).} At every matched budget AP-Q is worse than AR-ref on held-out and validation BPB, on all \CREOnePairs{} seed--budget pairs (Table~\ref{tab:e1}, Figure~\ref{fig:e1}a); H-E1b is contradicted ($\Delta=\CREOneHeldA$ at 88.6M) and H1 is supported. The means rise with tokens, but the per-seed contrast $\Delta(201.3\text{M})-\Delta(88.6\text{M})=\CREOneContrast$ \CREOneContrastCI{} leaves H-E1a inconclusive on held-out data (supported on validation); no crossover occurs in any of \CREOneResamples{} seed resamples; the pre-registered outcome is that exposure explains the fixed-time advantage. At 300\,s on the same seeds AP-Q processes \CREOneTokAP M tokens against \CREOneTokAR M and the recipes tie (held-out \CREOneTimeHeld{} \CREOneTimeHeldCI; validation \CREOneTimeVal), consistent with the seeds 0--2 re-runs (Welch difference \CREOneHistWelch). The panel's 201.3M result replicates on new seeds and another A40 (difference \CREOneReplDiff). Of the frozen benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:bench}) only BLiMP separates them (\CRAPQBLiMP{} vs \CRARBLiMP; $r=\CRCorrBLiMPHeld$ with held-out BPB).

\emph{Exploratory:} interpolating each seed's token curves at the tokens each recipe processed in 300\,s splits that tie into AP-Q's extra tokens (\CREOneExposure{} \CREOneExposureCI), its architecture at equal tokens (\CREOneArch{} \CREOneArchCI) and a schedule residual (\CREOneSched; Figure~\ref{fig:e1}b). AP-Q trains \CREOneRho$\times$ faster but needs about \CREOneK$\times$ \CREOneKCI{} AR-ref's tokens to match AR-ref at 300\,s: its throughput roughly buys back what it loses per token (split specified after the plan, before E1 results).

\paragraph{Two agents, one contract.} Both Pair-A agents also cut the batch and the depth (Figure~\ref{fig:lineage}b) and wrote down why: Codex expected ``higher token throughput within Contract T'' to offset a shallower model (A3), and Opus that a depth cut would give ``about 1.2x more updates and tokens'' (A3). After \CRCxAttempts{} attempts each, Codex's best validation BPB was \CRCxBestBPB{} (A\CRCxBestAttempt) and Opus's \CROpBestTwentyTwo{} (A\CROpBestTwentyTwoAttempt; later \CROpBestBPB{} at A\CROpBestAttempt) with three-layer models; one seed each, so we do not rank agents. Opus had \CROpBestSeven{} by A7 from batch and depth alone; its \CROpLateDone{} later valid attempts gained \CROpLateGain{} BPB, and \CROpLateInf{} of them are labelled inference changes. From A\CROpAdapterAttempt{} its adapter captured the decode CUDA graph inside the untimed prefill, and decode-only speed-up rose from \CROpPreAdapterDecode$\times$ to \CROpAdapterDecode$\times$ as prefill grew from \CROpPreAdapterPrefill{} to \CROpAdapterPrefill{} ms (Figure~\ref{fig:agents}b); Opus noted that ``Prefill time rose from 6.4 ms to 81 ms'' (A11) and treated prefill as ``diagnostic only'' (A21). Its frontier decodes \CROpDecodeLo--\CROpDecodeHi$\times$ faster than the baseline but only \CROpEtoELo--\CROpEtoEHi$\times$ end to end; Codex's prefill never exceeded \CRCxMaxPrefill{} ms and its two speeds coincide.

\paragraph{Opus at matched tokens (pre-registered).} Retrained at E1's 201.3M tokens and 1{,}536 updates, within 3\% of its own 300\,s exposure (\CROpTokLo--\CROpTokHi M tokens, \CROpUpdLo--\CROpUpdHi{} updates), all three Opus recipes are worse than AR-ref by \CRMTDLo{} to \CRMTDHi{} held-out BPB (each 95\% CI above \CRMTCIMin; Holm-adjusted $p\le\CRMTHolm$; \CRMTPairs{} of 9 seed pairs) and worse than AP-Q by \CRMTDAPLo{} to \CRMTDAPHi{} (CIs above \CRMTDAPCIMin); validation agrees. They trail AR-ref on BLiMP by \CRMTBlimpLoPts--\CRMTBlimpHiPts{} points and tie it on LAMBADA, PIQA and ARC-Easy. They train \CRMTRhoLo--\CRMTRhoHi$\times$ AR-ref's tokens per second but need over \CRMTKBound$\times$ its tokens for equal BPB at 201.3M (exploratory; AP-Q: \CRMTKAPQ$\times$); post hoc, Opus at 201.3M tokens (about its 300\,s exposure) ties AR-ref at 300\,s (\CRMTPostLo{} to \CRMTPostHi; CIs span 0).

\paragraph{Where the decode speed-up comes from (ablations).} We removed inference mechanisms from Opus's adapters (\CRIARuns{} protected-benchmark runs; every variant passed the correctness check with identical greedy tokens; Figure~\ref{fig:e1}c). Replaying each decode step as one CUDA graph accounts for \CRIAGraphLo--\CRIAGraphHi$\times$ of decode throughput and fusing its kernels \citep{ansel2024pytorch2} for \CRIAFusionLo--\CRIAFusionHi$\times$ (\CRIAJointLo--\CRIAJointHi$\times$ together); custom attention and a ring K/V cache add \CRIAAttnLo$\times$ and \CRIARingLo$\times$. Without graph or fusion the three-layer model decodes only \CRIAArchLo--\CRIAArchHi$\times$ faster than the baseline: batch-1 decode is bound by per-kernel overhead (per token, \CRIAKernBase{} kernels for the baseline and \CRIAKernFused{} for fused Opus, which the graph issues in \CRIALaunchGraph{} host calls). End to end the graph is worth only \CRIAGraphEtoELo--\CRIAGraphEtoEHi$\times$, because capturing it for each prompt takes \CRIACapture\% of the \CRIAPrefillLo--\CRIAPrefillHi{} ms prefill (baseline \CRIAPrefillBase{} ms).

\subsection{Primary Result}
Which recipe ``wins'' depends on the contract, through exposure. Under a 300\,s budget a program's throughput counts as much as its per-token quality, and every agent we ran pulled that lever: AP-Q and Opus's recipes train \CREOneRho$\times$ and \CRMTRhoLo--\CRMTRhoHi$\times$ faster than AR-ref. Per token, pre-registered retraining finds both worse (\CREOneMatchedLo{} to \CREOneMatchedHi{} and \CRMTDLo{} to \CRMTDHi{} held-out BPB); at equal time AP-Q ties AR-ref (\CREOneTimeHeldThree, \CREOneTimeHeldCI) and, post hoc, so does Opus. What survives is efficiency: at matched tokens AP-Q decodes \CRDecodeRatio$\times$ faster than AR-ref with \CRParamReduction\% fewer parameters, \CRMemoryReduction\% less memory and half the KV cache, and Opus decodes about \CRIATotalMed$\times$ faster than the baseline (\CRIAEtoEMed$\times$ end to end), mostly via graph replay and fusion on unchanged weights with an untimed per-prompt capture.

\subsection{Agent and Harness}\label{sec:agent}
% P2-02: written with and approved by the author (2026-11-10).
My role was to design the research framework, define the objectives, compute budgets and evaluation rules, and maintain the experimental infrastructure. The AI agents independently proposed hypotheses, modified Transformer architectures and training settings, and selected which candidates to explore. Every historical AutoPareto and AutoResearch attempt was proposed by \texttt{gpt-5.6-sol} at high reasoning effort, which operated the protected runner on the GPU pod over SSH, through the Codex desktop app or, for five seed-42 attempts, Cursor's agent (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). In the Pair-A harness (\S\ref{sec:pilot}), Codex CLI 0.153.0 ran \texttt{gpt-5.6-sol} at high reasoning in a fresh container for every call (600-second timeout), seeing five staged files and writing only \texttt{train.py}. The Opus arm ran \texttt{claude-opus-5-5} (effort \texttt{xhigh}) through Claude Code 2.1.318 on the host, with file tools only, no shell, and the same prompts, output schemas and two calls per attempt; it was isolated by CLI permissions, file modes and hash checks, tested by a canary before the campaign. Historical isolation relied on instructions plus hash checks: protected files were hashed before and after every attempt, and any change outside \texttt{train.py} rejected the run. No agent saw downstream results. All agent session logs are retained privately and not publicly released.

\subsection{Tools}
% P2-03: written with and approved by the author (2026-11-10).
All training and measurement ran in BF16 on NVIDIA A40 GPUs (several physical cards across eras); software per era is in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:env}. The panel, E1 and the Opus retraining used Python 3.10.18 with PyTorch 2.9.1+cu128. The Pair-A campaigns ran in a separate Python 3.12 runtime with PyTorch 2.8.0+cu128 (2.9.1 in the September pilot), and the inference ablations also used PyTorch 2.8.0. The discovery-era environment was not logged at run time; the repository's lockfile then pinned PyTorch 2.9.1+cu128. A trusted controller stages each candidate, checks source and parent SHA-256 hashes, runs training, verifies the checkpoint, calls the protected BPB and inference evaluators, and appends the outcome to the registry and lineage.

\subsection{Research Loop}\label{sec:loop}
% P2-04: written with and approved by the author (2026-11-10).
Each attempt follows the loop below (historical campaigns: runner functions \texttt{validate\_autopareto\_proposal} and \texttt{raw\_pareto\_rows}; Pair-A: \texttt{campaign\_controller.py}, \texttt{campaign.py}).
{\small\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item Stage the campaign view (this campaign's records and current frontier) and the parent source.
\item The agent returns a hypothesis, intended change, change category, parent identifier and a patch to \texttt{train.py}.
\item Validate: the parent must be on the current frontier (historical) or parent-eligible in the same arm (Pair-A); protected-file and source hashes must match; only \texttt{train.py} may change.
\item Train under the contract (300\,s; or $N^\star$ counted tokens in the Pair-A fixed-token arm).
\item Protected evaluation: validation BPB, cached/full-logit correctness, cached-decode throughput, peak allocated memory.
\item Append an immutable record (hypothesis, patch, hashes, metrics, failure text); failures consume the attempt.
\item Update the archive: historically, a completed candidate is retained if neither another completed candidate nor the registered same-seed baseline dominates it on raw BPB, decode and memory (no noise margin); the agent labelled each outcome, and the runner rejected any label that disagreed with this rule.
\item The next attempt chooses its parent among retained (historical) or parent-eligible (Pair-A) records.
\end{enumerate}}
Without noise margins the archive admits noise-level trade-offs: A09 and A10 build the same model, yet A10 was retained. In Pair-A every quality-valid record was parent-eligible even when its speed was uncertain.

\subsection{Human Interventions}\label{sec:interventions}
% P2-06: written with and approved by the author (2026-11-12).
I chose the objectives, the 300-second contract, the matched-exposure design, the panel, E1 and Opus-test recipes, the comparators and the interpretation. I made or approved each intervention below; AI coding assistants carried out several at my instruction. (i) \emph{Seed-42 AutoPareto rollback.} Attempt 4 failed the cache-correctness check and was labelled dominated, and attempts 5--8 ran on 2026-07-28. When the check was found faulty and repaired, I discarded attempts 5--8 because they had followed a frontier that wrongly excluded attempt 4 (ids 88--90 are absent); attempt 4 was re-measured on a recreated checkpoint and changed to retained, and attempt 5 was replayed. Attempt 10 was re-adjudicated after an FP32 evaluator amendment, and the program text changed after attempt 4. I started seeds 0 and 1 after reviewing seed 42. (ii) \emph{AutoResearch.} In the 50-attempt campaign 27 trained candidates were discarded by a cache-correctness check later found faulty; AR-ref comes from a 5-attempt follow-up under a revised program. (iii) \emph{Pair-A, Codex.} Inference-adapter amendments, requeues after pod or provider failures, configuration changes before launch, and inconsistent failure adjudication in a later restart. (iv) \emph{Pair-A, Opus.} A defect in the protected FP32 correctness adjudication was fixed mid-campaign and the affected attempt re-run; the agent-call timeout was raised from 600 to 900\,s from attempt 9 (Codex ran at 600\,s); attempt 9 was charged as a candidate failure; the arm was extended from 25 attempts to 25 valid results (27 attempts; we also report the frozen first 25); timed-out agent calls before any candidate were requeued; its recorded attempts ran on two A40s (A1; A2--A27), as Codex's did (A1--A9; A10--A22). (v) \emph{Failure accounting.} A17's tokenizer failure consumed a seed-0 attempt; the identical seed-42 failure was relabelled as setup. (vi) \emph{E1} ran on a different A40 from the environment check (E0), as disclosed in its record. (vii) \emph{Opus retraining}: a scripted driver mapping and storage export, registered with the plan. The AP-Q lineage (seed 0) has no recorded scientific intervention: its program, evaluator and records were not changed. Interventions whose authorship cannot be reconstructed are excluded from claims of autonomous contribution.

\subsection{Significance of result}
\humanauthor[P2-12]{Matched-exposure verification shows that the quality ranking depends on the contract, while the deployment-efficiency result survives every contract we tested. The pre-registered token sweep finds no budget at which AP-Q is better per token, so its fixed-time lead was exposure. Agents from two model families found the same lever and wrote it into their hypotheses: under a time budget, a smaller program trains on more data. The second pushed it further, was worse still per token, and took most of its decode gain from graph replay whose capture the metric did not time. A benchmark that ranks agents under a throughput-coupled budget is partly ranking how fast their candidates train; reported quality gains should be rechecked under controlled exposure and speed gains under end-to-end timing.}

\subsection{Interpretability}
\humanauthor[P2-13]{Every preserved attempt record retains the agent's stated hypothesis, its intended change, the applied patch, the parent identifier, source and parent hashes, the status, the raw metrics and the failure text, so a headline number can be traced to a source file, to the edit that produced it and to the hypothesis recorded before it (Appendix~\ref{app:decisions}). Agent session logs survive for every campaign (privately for the historical ones, which contain infrastructure access details), and change categories are labels supplied with each proposal. The record also exposes gaps between intent and execution: the recorded intended changes for A7 and A8 predict widths 384$\to$320$\to$256, whereas the width rounding kept A7 at 384 and took A8 from 384 to 256.}

\subsection{Limitations}
\humanauthor[P2-14]{One GPU type (A40) and small models; no scale-transfer, cross-GPU or energy claim. Matched-token verification compares complete recipes, so architecture and optimization changes stay bundled, and in E1 tokens and updates still change together (a batch-varied follow-up was not run). E1 resolves the matched-token gap with three seeds but not the slope of $\Delta$ with tokens; its exposure--architecture split is exploratory and assumes piecewise-linear curves in log tokens. Selection narrowed after discovery, and a pre-registered full-size candidate (exp 109) was never confirmed. Each Pair-A agent ran one seed, so we do not rank agents; the Codex arm stopped after 22 attempts, and the Opus arm had a longer agent timeout and two extra attempts (neither changes a conclusion; Appendix~\ref{app:faq}). The Opus retraining covers one budget and replaces its time-fraction learning-rate progress with the token fraction; the ablations measure one workload (1{,}024-token prompt, 128 tokens) on one host, their factors interact, and graph replay and fusion were not applied to the baseline or AR-ref. Historical comparators differ in search space, budget, seed and failure history; historical agent logs are private. Downstream records hold task-level scores, not per-item outputs. Proprietary agent behaviour limits exact reproduction.}

\subsection{Pair-A: controlled agent campaigns}\label{sec:pilot}
\humanauthor[P2-15]{We piloted a prospective test of H2. Two arms whose programs differ only in the contract paragraph (T: 300\,s; N: $N^\star=88{,}604{,}672$ counted tokens, the largest multiple of 524,288 not above the median of three baseline calibration runs) started from identical bytes at seed 3. Five Codex launch lineages ran between 2026-09-05 and 2026-09-17, with protocol changes between them; Part~\ref{partone} reports the 2026-09-14 restart (22 attempts). In the earlier lineage reported at submission, 2 of its 4 fixed-time attempts are invalid: when a candidate's training loop ended before the trusted stop, the harness re-executed the training body and checkpointed a freshly initialized model (candidate-reported step 0 against 1,264 observed updates; BPB 3.2159). The controller still marked these runs quality-valid and parent-eligible, and the agent's next hypothesis blamed its learning rates. Later restarts disagree on whether either arm explores batch size, so no contract effect on agent behaviour can be estimated. The harness used for the Opus arm treats an early normal exit as a candidate failure.}

\humanauthor[P2-16]{\textbf{What the speed metric does not time.} The Opus arm inherited the Codex arm's frozen contract, schedule, baseline, data, evaluator and Pareto rule; besides the agent backend, its isolation, call timeout and attempt budget (\S\ref{sec:interventions}), only the early-exit repair and a mid-campaign FP32 adjudication fix changed. Its largest decode gains came from inference-adapter edits that capture the decode CUDA graph, and later compiled regions, inside \texttt{prefill}, which the decode-only metric leaves untimed; end-to-end speed, recorded but not scored, rose far less (Figure~\ref{fig:agents}b). The ablations confirm the mechanism: the graph supplies \CRIAGraphLo--\CRIAGraphHi$\times$ of decode speed but its capture takes \CRIACapture\% of prefill (Figure~\ref{fig:e1}c). This is within the permitted surface, so we report both speeds rather than relabel it. Two further gaps surfaced: the outputs of the timed decode windows are not checked, and the correctness prefixes (64 and 256 tokens) do not exercise a prefill ring buffer at 1{,}024 tokens. A scored end-to-end or prefill-inclusive metric, and output checks inside timed windows, belong in the harness.}

\section{Broader Impact}\label{partthree}
Resource contracts are part of the scientific specification of autonomous research. Neither contract is wrong: a time budget asks which program gives the best model per GPU-second, a token budget which gives the best model per training token, and a paper should say which question its scores answer. When an agent controls architecture and training together, a time allowance changes how much data a faster candidate sees, and a token allowance removes that pathway while letting update count and compute vary. The measurement contract matters in the same way: an agent rewarded for a decode-only number will improve the decode-only number, here largely by moving graph capture into the untimed prefill. Agents read a contract the way an optimizer reads a loss: agents from both model families pulled the throughput lever within their first three attempts, and Opus moved work into the untimed prefill while noting its cost. Contract designers should assume that an agent will find every degree of freedom the contract leaves open. Complete trajectories should retain crashes, unstable measurements and scientifically poor but technically completed runs, and parent-eligibility rules must be recorded because they change the search policy.

\textbf{A contract card.} We propose that autonomous-ML papers report, for every scored result: (1) the stopping rule and the learning-rate progress variable; (2) the seeds used for discovery and for comparison; (3) tokens, optimizer updates and FLOPs per scored run; (4) session-throughput variation under time contracts; (5) what each speed or memory metric times and excludes (for example prefill); (6) the selection funnel from attempts to reported recipes, with dates; and (7) harness invariants such as checkpoint lineage and failure semantics. Our own harness once accepted a checkpoint of an untrained model as quality-valid; invariant checks belong in the protected boundary, not in post-hoc audits.

\textbf{Follow-up work, not reported here.} A batch-varied test that separates tokens from updates (E1b); a matched-token test of the full-size seed-42 candidate exp 109 (E4); a replicated, seed-paired contract study under one frozen harness with prefill-inclusive speed scoring (E3); and a small scaling pilot. The environment check (E0, Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), the token sweep (E1), the Opus retraining and the inference ablations have been run and are reported above.

Efficiency may reduce the cost of an individual experiment or deployment, yet cheaper experiments can increase total experimentation; we report no energy benefit because the artifacts contain no campaign-wide energy accounting. Small-model benchmark results do not establish safe or reliable use in consequential settings. Human accountability grows with experiment volume: humans set objectives, repair infrastructure, decide which failures are scientific and interpret evidence, and an auditable record should keep the human question, the agent's prediction, the source edit, the measured outcome and the retrospective explanation distinguishable.
\label{partthreeend}

\section{Questions a reader may ask}\label{app:faq}
\textbf{Is AP-Q simply a worse model?} Per training token, yes (Table~\ref{tab:e1}). Per GPU-second it ties AR-ref (300\,s, E1) while being \CRParamReduction\% smaller, decoding \CRDecodeRatio$\times$ faster and using \CRMemoryReduction\% less inference memory. Which matters depends on whether compute time or data is the scarce resource.

\textbf{Why not use a fixed-token contract from the start?} A fixed-time contract is the autoresearch standard and answers a practical question: the best model from a fixed amount of GPU time. It is not wrong, but it scores a program's throughput and its per-token quality together. A fixed-token contract isolates per-token quality but lets compute and update count vary. We report both.

\textbf{Why did every agent shrink the model?} Under 300\,s, BPB improves with more updates and tokens, and a smaller model gets more of both. The agents' hypotheses say so (Table~\ref{tab:reasoning}). E1 prices the trade for AP-Q: it trains \CREOneRho$\times$ faster but needs about \CREOneK$\times$ AR-ref's tokens for equal quality, so at 300\,s the two cancel.

\textbf{Are three seeds enough?} For the matched-token gap, yes: \CREOneMatchedLo{} to \CREOneMatchedHi{} held-out BPB against a within-recipe seed SD of at most \CREOneMaxSD, on all \CREOnePairs{} seed--budget pairs, and the panel result replicates on other seeds and another A40. For the slope of the gap with tokens, no: H-E1a is inconclusive on held-out data.

\textbf{Did Opus game the decode metric?} It stayed within the permitted surface: the adapter had to compute the same outputs, and every variant passed the correctness check with identical greedy tokens. Its records describe graph replay as a fix for launch-bound decode and note the prefill cost (Table~\ref{tab:reasoning}), so it was the metric's definition, not a rule violation, that rewarded the placement. We report decode-only and end-to-end speed side by side and recommend prefill-inclusive scoring (\S\ref{sec:pilot}).

\textbf{Is the Codex--Opus comparison fair?} The arms share the baseline, seed, prompt bytes, output schemas, contract and Pareto rule; they differ in agent-call timeout (600 vs 900\,s), isolation and attempt count (\CRCxAttempts{} vs \CROpAttempts), and each ran one seed. At \CRCxAttempts{} attempts each the best validation BPBs are \CRCxBestBPB{} and \CROpBestTwentyTwo. We draw no agent ranking; the arms show the same exposure strategy and supply recipes for retraining.

\textbf{Does the Opus extension to \CROpAttempts{} attempts change anything?} No. Its best validation BPB (\CROpBestFrozen) came within the frozen first 25 attempts; the frozen frontier (\CROpFrontierFrozen) shares A23 and A24 with the retrained set, and the third retrained recipe, A27, matches A23's held-out gap to AR-ref to three decimals (Table~\ref{tab:e1}).

\textbf{Could the time-to-token mapping handicap Opus's recipes?} The mapping changed only driver lines (clock, stop, progress); model and optimizer code stayed byte-identical. The 201.3M tokens and 1{,}536 updates are within 3\% of Opus's own 300\,s runs (\CROpTokLo--\CROpTokHi M tokens, \CROpUpdLo--\CROpUpdHi{} updates), so its recipes were scored at almost the exposure they were tuned for, and an AR-ref drift control reproduced E1 within \CRMTDriftAbs{} BPB.

\textbf{Why are the zero-shot differences small?} ARC-Easy, PIQA and LAMBADA vary by about two points across recipes, close to their seed SD (typically \CRBenchSDMed, at most \CRBenchSDMax{} points). BLiMP is the one task that separates recipes, and it tracks held-out BPB (Table~\ref{tab:bench}).

\textbf{Were the reported recipes cherry-picked?} The funnel and its dates are in Table~\ref{tab:funnel}: the representative rule predates the first attempt, the global selector preceded the seeds 0--2 confirmation, and the panel was assembled after it. A full-size candidate (exp 109) was never confirmed. E1, the Opus retraining and the ablations were pre-registered.

\textbf{Tokens or updates?} E1 and the Opus retraining match both, so the gaps are per token and per update; which of the two drives the fixed-time gain was not separated (E1b, not run).

\textbf{Does this transfer to larger models or other GPUs?} Untested: one GPU type and recipes of 18--50M parameters.
